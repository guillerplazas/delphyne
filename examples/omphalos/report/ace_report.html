Playbooks that did not pay

We implemented ACE — Agentic Context Engineering — inside the Rocq proving agent, ran the paper's label-free offline arm on both held-out partitions, and measured no effect on what gets proved. The interesting part is why, and what the measurement turned up instead.

00 What was asked, and what came back

ACE (arXiv:2510.04618) treats an LLM's context as an evolving playbook: a list of itemised bullets that a pipeline of three roles grows from experience. It reports +10.6% on agent benchmarks and, crucially for us, works without ground-truth labels — from execution feedback alone. Our verifier is execution feedback of the best possible kind, so the fit looked exact.

It was implemented in full, evaluated on validation and on the clean test partition, and ablated along two of the paper's own axes. On our benchmark it changes nothing that can be detected: the same problems are proved, for statistically indistinguishable money. Three findings came out of the measurement anyway.

The three findings

1. ACE is a null here, twice over. Validation and test both land at zero detectable difference in solves. Test is zero discordant — the same sixteen problems, one for one.

2. The mechanism is real even though the outcome is not. The playbook roughly halves generated output tokens on both partitions and cuts the number of problems lost to invented lemma names. It substitutes retrieved knowledge for re-derived reasoning; that substitution just does not reach the margin where proofs are won or lost.

3. Four of our nine remaining failures had broken prompts. They were being asked to prove statements about symbols the prompt never showed them. Fixing that recovered one of eight cells and broke none — so the harness was wrong, and the ceiling is real anyway.

01 The pipeline, and what it cost to build

Three roles, all on the same model. The Generator is the existing agentic prover with the playbook appended to its system prompt. The Reflector reads the whole trajectory plus the verifier's verdict and returns a diagnosis. The Curator turns that diagnosis into ADD-only delta operations, which deterministic Python — never the model — merges into the playbook. That last division is the paper's central claim: monolithic LLM rewrites of a context erode it, so the merge is kept out of the model's hands.

In Delphyne terms the whole thing is two queries, one strategy subclass and a driver. It touches no frozen code: the ACE prover is a subclass of the agentic query, so it gets its own templates and its own cache namespace, and its few-shot examples are fetched from the baseline's bucket and render byte-identically. That is not a stylistic claim — it is checked, by replaying every archived run under a cache mode that raises on any miss.

02 What the playbook learned

Twenty problems, one epoch, execution feedback only. The playbook grows monotonically to 28 bullets (~2k tokens): eleven pitfalls, ten tactic rules, five lemma facts, two strategy notes. They are specific in the way the paper wants — Rsqr versus x ^ 2 before reaching for nra; sqrt_def's side conditions; goals stated over C need ring, not lra.

Context collapse did not happen here

The rewrite arm's playbook grows — it does not erode. That is worth saying plainly, because preventing collapse is ACE's central argument for putting the merge in deterministic code. Their collapse appears at 18,000 tokens and sixty-plus steps; ours is a tenth of that size over twenty steps, and at this scale a model re-emitting its own playbook has no trouble carrying it forward.

So the deterministic merge is earning something other than what it was sold for. What it does buy, unconditionally, is bookkeeping the LLM arm structurally cannot have: stable bullet ids, dedup counters, and an auditable diff per step. That is worth keeping — but on this benchmark it is not what stands between a working playbook and a collapsed one.

The Curator's restraint is worth noting: on clean solves it frequently adds nothing, which is the intended behaviour and the reason the playbook stays small enough to inject at all.

What the Reflector is for

The paper's first ablation drops the Reflector and lets the Curator read the raw trajectory. Re-run here, it produces a playbook of almost exactly the same size — and a visibly different one:

Reading a trajectory directly, the Curator writes down twice as many concrete lemma facts and no strategy bullets at all. The Reflector's contribution is not volume but abstraction: it converts "this lemma existed" into "this failure mode recurs, and here is how to spend a turn on it." The tagged column is zero by construction — with no Reflector nothing produces bullet tags, so the helpful/harmful counters never move, and any future pruning built on them would have nothing to work with.

A robustness cost the paper does not mention

The Curator answers in a fenced YAML block, and YAML reads an unquoted : inside a scalar as a mapping — so a bullet containing assert `0 <= (c : R)` fails to parse, and can fail on every retry. It killed a 60-step run outright before being handled. One step in twenty hit it here. Such a step now contributes nothing and is counted in steps.csv rather than raising, so the parse-failure rate stays visible instead of becoming an invisible hole in the playbook.

Why no p-value here can be small

An exact sign test needs 6 discordant cells, all one way, to reach p < 0.05. Identical configurations in this project disagree on 1–3 cells run to run. Validation produced 2 discordant cells and test produced 0 — so these are nulls in the strict sense of no effect was detected, and the experiment could not have detected a small one. What it does exclude is a large effect: ACE is not converting handfuls of problems.

That last point is why this report never quotes a pooled total as a result. Pooled, validation says ACE costs 13.8% more and test says it costs 8.5% less. Both are the same heavy tail talking.

04 Where the money actually moves

Underneath the null there is a consistent, reproducible mechanism. On both partitions the playbook roughly halves generated output tokens and slightly raises the share of input served from cache. The agent reads more and thinks less.

PartitionMedian output tokensCached input share

On this rate card that substitution should be a saving: output bills at $1.20/M against $0.02/M for cached input, sixty to one. It shows up on test (median cell 0.74×) and washes out on validation. The trade is real; its sign at the bottom line is not stable.

The budget question the $0.05 cap never asked

The canonical per-problem cap is $0.05. The median solved cell costs $0.0023 — twenty times less. The cap only ever binds on failures, so does a learned context pay under a hard budget? was never actually put to either arm. It can be answered exactly and for free: a capped run is a strict prefix of a recorded one, so every cell's outcome under every cap is computable from the caches we already have.

What the curve says

At the tightest budgets — where only one or two attempts fit — ACE is ahead, and it is the only region where the discordant cells all point one way (3–0 at $0.0005, p = 0.25). That is the front-loading effect the paper predicts, and it is directional evidence, not a result: three cells is half the six needed.

In the mid-range the sign reverses: the per-turn playbook tax costs the agent an attempt it would otherwise have afforded. Above ~$0.04 both arms saturate and the question stops mattering. A playbook is a bet that knowledge is worth more than one more try — and that bet only pays when a try is expensive relative to the budget.

05 How the agent fails, with and without a playbook

Solve counts are identical, so the interesting comparison is the failure taxonomy: same verifier, same problems, different errors.

Invented lemma names fall from 7 problems to 4, and incomplete proofs from 4 to 3. That is exactly what the playbook's lemma bullets target, and it is the clearest evidence that the context is being read and used. Syntax errors move the other way. The agent is failing differently, not less.

06 The finding that was not about ACE

Chasing the ceiling turned up a bug in the benchmark harness. It is a real defect, cheap to fix, and it moved the numbers far less than it looked like it would — which is itself the useful part.

parse_problem captured only Require, Import and Open Scope lines when building the prompt's preamble. Forty-two of the 488 miniF2F files also declare a Definition or Fixpoint their theorem then references — and those declarations were silently dropped. Verification was unaffected, because Rocq loads the real file; only the model was kept in the dark. Here is what it was actually asked to prove:

numbertheory_nckeqnm1ckpnm1ckm1 — the whole prompt preamble

Require Import Arith.

Theorem …: forall (n k : nat), 0 < k <= n ->
  choose n k = choose (n-1) k + choose (n-1) (k-1).

choose is defined ten lines above in the source file, via factorials. The model never saw it. No playbook, and no amount of reasoning effort, can close that goal.

Five of our sixty benchmark problems are affected. The canonical configuration has nine distinct failures across all three partitions, and four of them are these problems — two of the three hard validation failures, and two of the four test failures. That looked like nearly half the apparent ceiling being an artefact. It was not:

The measurement, not the intuition

Showing the definitions converted one cell of eight and broke none; the control problem — solved despite the missing definition — stayed solved. So these problems are hard for reasons beyond the missing preamble: choose still needs reasoning about truncated natural-number division, and the AM–GM inequality is genuinely difficult.

The lesson generalises past this bug. A defect that plausibly explains four failures explained one, and the only way to know was to spend $0.21 finding out. The ceiling is mostly real.

The fix is gated behind a flag that defaults to off, so every archived run still replays byte-identically and the change is a measured arm rather than a silent re-baselining. It should be turned on for future work regardless of the small gain: a prompt that references a symbol it never defines is simply wrong, and leaving it wrong contaminates every failure analysis run over it.

07 What we would need to see an effect

The honest summary is that this benchmark cannot currently resolve an effect of ACE's size, and the reasons are structural rather than incidental:

  • The ceiling is low and hard. Fifteen of twenty validation problems are solved at both seeds and three at neither. Only two are genuinely in play, so more seeds resample noise — the power lever is more problems, not more repetitions.
  • The paper predicts this regime. Its own limitations section says ACE helps least where a handful of rules suffice. Our system prompt already carries a hand-mined pitfalls section — six lessons a human distilled from failure traces. ACE automates that job, and largely re-derived what was already there.
  • The budget never bound. The one regime where the playbook is directionally ahead is a cap twenty times tighter than the one we ship.

Three levers remain, in the order we would pull them. A much larger adaptation corpus: the twenty training problems were solved nineteen times out of twenty, so the Reflector spent most of its budget reflecting on successes. A balanced 100-problem corpus is generated and the arm is wired, but not yet run — it opens on eleven AIME problems that burn the full request budget, and needs about four hours of wall-clock rather than the forty minutes we modelled. Selective injection, so an algebra problem stops paying for the induction bullets; the machinery is in place (injection=top<k>). And generator-side citation of the bullets actually applied, which would turn the helpful/harmful counters from advisory metadata into something that can drive pruning — today, in the no-Reflector arm, they never move at all.

For the platform question

As evidence for the thesis's second research question, this went well. A three-role pipeline with its own queries, prompts, budgets, caches and ablation arms was added as a strictly additive extension — no frozen file changed behaviour, and that claim is proved on every commit by replaying the archives under a cache mode that raises on a miss. The framework made a negative result cheap to obtain and impossible to fudge, which is most of what an experimental platform is for.
